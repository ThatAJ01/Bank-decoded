View 1 · Regulatory

How regulators' numbers see you

Think of a bank as a water system. Deposits are water coming in; loans are water going out into pipes that might burst. Regulators ask two questions: can the water leak out too fast? (liquidity) and how much of the bank's own money sits behind the pipes? (capital). Each question gets a dial.

Dial 1 · Liquidity: can the bank pay people back in a crisis?

Why does the regulator care? Banks lend long (a mortgage lasts 25 years) but most deposits can be taken out any day. In a panic, many people withdraw at once (a “bank run”). Regulators therefore make banks hold an emergency cushion of cash-like assets. The cushion is sized by assuming a share of each type of deposit leaves within 30 days. Money that historically leaves slowly (insured, from long-standing customers) gets a low assumed share; flighty money gets a higher one.

The test is called the LCR . The assumed leaving share is the run-off rate.

Your deposits and unused credit lines
How should the bank classify your deposits?

The rule for “stable”

A deposit is stable (5%) if it is covered by the deposit guarantee (up to €100,000 per person per bank ) and either your salary is paid into that account or you are an established customer. Other retail deposits get 10% or more . A deposit locked for more than about 30 days is not expected to leave in the window (0%) . Unused credit lines count 5% , because people may draw on them in a crisis.

LCR is measured for the whole bank, not per customer. This is a slice for illustration. Loan repayments are inflows that partly offset outflows (not shown).

Stable funding: the one-year test

Why a second liquidity test? The LCR only looks 30 days ahead. A bank could pass it and still fund 25-year mortgages with money that must be rolled over every few months. The NSFR asks: over one year, is the supply of stable funding (ASF) at least the demand for it from loans (RSF)?

Dial 2 · Risk-weighted assets: how risky is each loan?

Why risk weights exist

A bank must hold its own money (capital) to absorb loan losses, so that losses fall on shareholders instead of depositors or taxpayers. But a €100 loan to a safe borrower and a €100 loan to a risky one should not need the same cushion. A risk weight scales each loan: €100 × 35% = €35 of “risk-weighted assets” (RWA). Capital requirements are then a % of RWA, so lower weight means less capital needed.

Why fixed weights? Under the Standardised Approach (SA), regulators publish the weights so every bank applies the same rule and supervisors can compare banks easily. The weights come from the global Basel standard, calibrated on historical loss data and negotiated politically, and written into EU law by the CRR. They are deliberately simple, so they describe types of loans, not you personally.

Analogy: insurers charge by car type and age band, not by how carefully each driver actually drives.

Dial 3 · Capital: how much of the bank's own money must sit behind your loans?

Why so many layers?

The bank's capital must be at least a % of its RWA. The law sets a base, then adds extra layers for different reasons:

  • Pillar 1 minimum: the same for every bank (4.5% / 6% / 8%).
  • SREP / Pillar 2 requirement (P2R): the supervisor reviews each bank (the SREP) and adds capital for risks the minimum misses.
  • Conservation buffer: a cushion everyone must hold, meant to be used in bad times (if it is dipped into, dividends and bonuses are restricted).
  • Countercyclical buffer (CCyB): a national authority raises it when credit is growing too fast (build up in good times, release in bad times).
  • Systemic risk buffer (SyRB): a national add-on for structural risks, e.g. a very concentrated or property-heavy economy.
  • O-SII / G-SII buffer: extra for banks so big that their failure would damage the economy.
  • Pillar 2 guidance (P2G): the supervisor's “please keep this extra”. Not binding, but banks stay above it.

What this means for you

  • Staying put is valued. A salary account, a long relationship and an insured balance make your money “stable”.
  • Mind the €100,000 limit per bank if safety matters. Above it, money is uninsured and treated as less stable. Try Aiko's preset.
  • A bigger down payment lowers the bank's capital need. Try Jonas's preset and compare the average risk weight.
  • Pay cards in full. “Transactors” carry a lower risk weight. Whether a bank passes that on varies.
  • Unused limits are not free for the bank. They count as potential exposure.

View 2 · Risk

How the bank sees you as a source of risk

View 1 is what the regulator counts. View 2 is what the bank watches: will you repay, how do you behave, and is everything above board?

Capital vs. the bank's own viewThe Standardised Approach gives similar loans the same fixed weight (View 1). But banks still estimate your chance of default for approval, pricing, monitoring and accounting provisions (IFRS 9). That is what this page shows.

Credit risk: PD, LGD and EAD

The bike-lending test You lend a friend your bike. PD (probability of default): how likely is it that it never comes back? EAD (exposure at default): what is it worth? LGD (loss given default): if it goes missing, what share of its value do you lose after anything you recover? Expected loss = PD × LGD × EAD.

PD is a probability: it can be anything from 0% to 100%. Mortgages to stable borrowers: often well under 1%. Unsecured consumer loans: typically 1–5%. Risky or already-troubled loans: tens of percent. At 100% the default has effectively happened (IFRS 9 stage 3).

Slip-up simulator

Why stages? Under IFRS 9, banks must set aside money for losses before they happen, in three stages: 1 normal loans (expected loss over 12 months), 2 credit risk has risen significantly (30+ days late is the standard backstop ), expected loss over the whole remaining life, 3 credit-impaired, usually 90+ days late (the same 90 days appear in the EU definition of default ).

Behaviour and concentration

▲ Early warning

Regular overdraft use, salary stops arriving, card at its limit with minimum payments. The bank may lower limits, reach out or tighten lending.

● Watch

Several credit applications close together; large sudden withdrawals; chasing the best savings rate (money treated as less stable).

✓ Reassuring

On-time payments, savings buffer, card paid in full, long relationship with salary paid in.

Early repayment and concentration

Early repayment costs the bank interest it planned to earn. Fees must be fair: for mortgages not above the bank's financial loss (national caps apply) ; for consumer loans generally capped at 1% of the amount repaid early (0.5% if under a year remains) .

Concentration: a bank with mostly mortgages in one country is exposed to that housing market. It may tighten rules even if you personally are fine. Retail portfolios must stay diversified to keep the 75% weight.

What this means for you

  • Payment history is your strongest signal. Use direct debits. One 30-day delay can push a loan into stage 2.
  • A savings cushion of 3 to 6 months of expenses lowers perceived risk even if untouched.
  • Use credit as a tool, not a lifeline. Persistent overdraft use is an early stress signal.
  • Talk early if you struggle. Banks are expected to offer help. Waiting until day 90 costs both sides more.
  • Expect routine KYC questions and keep documents for large sums.

View 3 · Lending

What it takes to get a loan, and how your rate is set

Two questions: can you afford it, and is the paperwork right? (approval) and what does lending to you cost the bank, plus profit? (price). Like a taxi fare: fuel, risk of breakdown, the car's cost, the driver's wage and a small profit.

Part 1 · What the bank needs from you

  1. Decide and pre-check. Compare offers using the standard European information sheet (ESIS for mortgages, SECCI for consumer loans).
  2. Documents. ID, proof of address, last 3 payslips (self-employed: 2 to 3 years of accounts), employment contract, 3 to 6 months of statements, list of debts. Mortgage: purchase contract, valuation, proof of down payment, insurance.
  3. Identity and checks. KYC, anti-money-laundering, credit-register search.
  4. Affordability. Income against expenses and debts, plus an interest-rate stress test.
  5. Collateral. Independent valuation; check LTV against bank and national limits.
  6. Decision and offer. You can ask for human review of an automated consumer-credit decision.
  7. Signing and after. Funds released, monitoring, early-repayment rights, help if you struggle.

The legal duty to assess whether you can repay comes from the Mortgage Credit Directive and CCD2 ; EBA guidelines on loan origination explain how. The assessment must focus on repayment from income, not only on the home's value. There is no EU-wide LTV or debt-to-income limit; those are national or bank rules, so check your country.

Why stress-test? Rates can rise during a 25-year loan. Banks must consider that, so they test whether you could still pay at a higher rate .

Part 2 · How the bank builds your rate

Why a formula? A loan costs the bank money in six ways. The rate has to cover all of them, and competition decides how much profit is left.

Rate = funding cost + liquidity premium + expected loss + capital cost + operating cost + margin.

Part 3 · Where the Standardised Approach comes in

Why the same risk weight can still mean different pricesThe Standardised Approach gives all regulatory-retail borrowers 75%. So a safer borrower doesn't need less capital, but has lower expected loss. Banks add their own view of your risk in the price. In personal-loan mode, move the PD: the capital cost stays fixed while the rate moves.
  1. Risk weight drives price. Higher LTV → more capital → higher rate.
  2. Capital is rationed. Loans that use a lot of capital for thin margins may be priced up or limited.
  3. Deposits can lower your price. Stable deposits are cheap funding that also helps the liquidity ratios, so banks may reward salary and savings with a small discount.
Other price factors

Competition squeezes margins. Fixed rates follow swap rates for the fixed period; variable rates follow Euribor plus a margin. Fees are spread over the loan's life in the bank's accounts, so fee and rate can trade off. Early-repayment fees reflect the cost of unwinding funding. Energy-efficient homes may get discounts as a bank choice, not an automatic capital discount.

What this means for you

  • Run your own stress test first. Can you pay if the rate is 2 points higher? Banks check.
  • Gather documents before applying. Missing papers are the most common delay.
  • Bigger down payment, lower LTV, cheaper loan. Compare offers by APRC (mortgages) or APR (consumer loans), which include fees.
  • Ask what your relationship earns you (salary account, savings, insurance). Get it in writing and check what you'd lose by cancelling a product.
  • Don't hide debts or income issues. Credit registers make it likely to be found.
  • You can request human review of an automated consumer-credit decision.
